El presente: tiempo, aspecto y modo

A reference for every present-tense form in Spanish — five inflected tenses plus six aspectual periphrases — with every standard name (RAE, Andrés Bello, and pedagogical traditions), notes on Peninsular and Latin American variation, and a self-check identification quiz.

Tres sistemas, un solo verbo

Before You Start

Every conjugated Spanish verb form is the intersection of three independent grammatical systems at once. Confusing them is the single most common source of confusion when students first meet terms like antepresente or perífrasis aspectual — so before looking at any individual form, it helps to separate the three questions each one is answering.

Tiempo · Tense

¿Cuándo?

Locates the action on the timeline relative to the moment of speech: past, present, or future. This is the axis most students already know from English.

Aspecto · Aspect

¿Cómo se desarrolla?

Describes the internal shape of the action, independent of when it happens: is it complete or still unfolding? A single event or a repeated habit? Finished a moment ago, or still in progress right now?

Modo · Mood

¿Qué actitud tiene el hablante?

Reflects the speaker's stance toward the action: asserted as real and factual (indicative), or filtered through doubt, emotion, volition, or unreality (subjunctive).

Why this matters for the present tense specifically: Spanish has one present tense but at least five inflected present-tense forms plus four common periphrastic constructions — because tense stays constant (everything below is anchored to "now") while aspect and mood keep varying. Hablo, he hablado, estoy hablando, hable, and haya hablado are all, in a sense, "present" — they just package the present moment differently.

Aspecto perfectivo vs. imperfectivo

Within the present-time forms below, aspect divides roughly into three families. Recognizing which family a form belongs to is more useful for production than memorizing labels in isolation.

Aspect familyWhat it signalsPresent-tense forms that carry it
ImperfectivoOngoing, habitual, or open-ended — no inherent endpoint in view.Presente simple; presente de subjuntivo; perífrasis habitual
Perfectivo / resultativoViewed as a completed, bounded unit with continuing relevance to now.Presente perfecto; presente perfecto de subjuntivo; perífrasis terminativa
Durativo / progresivoCaught mid-unfolding, in the exact instant of speaking.Presente progresivo; perífrasis acumulativa; perífrasis continuativa
ProspectivoLocated after now — not begun, but intention or likelihood is already present.Perífrasis prospectiva (ir a + infinitivo)
Incoativo / ingresivoThe onset of an action — the moment it starts.Ponerse a, echarse a, romper a, empezar a + infinitivo
Two honest caveats about the table above. First, Spanish's morphological aspect contrast lives in the past tense (pretérito vs. imperfecto), not the present — the present has no aspectually contrastive endings. What's grouped here as "aspect families" is aspect expressed periphrastically (through auxiliary verbs) plus the inherent aspect of the verb itself. The RAE treats perífrasis verbales in a separate chapter from tiempos verbales for exactly this reason. Second, the RAE classifies the pretérito perfecto compuesto (he hablado) as a past tense, not a present one. Collecting it here under "the present" is a deliberate teaching device — it groups everything anchored to now — not the standard taxonomy. Both simplifications are useful; just don't mistake either for what a reference grammar will tell you.

A useful diagnostic: simple vs. compound. Spanish tenses come in one-word simple forms (hablo) and auxiliary + participle compound forms (he hablado). Compound forms always express anteriority — an action prior to a reference point — which is exactly why Andrés Bello's system prefixes them with ante- (antepresente = "before the present," i.e., completed relative to now).

Beyond the Basics

Other jobs the simple present quietly does

Because the simple present is Spanish's default, unmarked, imperfective form, it moonlights in several roles that surprise students who only expect it to mean "I do / I am doing":

Presente histórico narrates a past event as if it were unfolding live, for dramatic immediacy — Cristóbal Colón llega a América en 1492. Presente por futuro reports a scheduled or near-certain future event — El tren sale a las ocho mañana. Presente de mandato issues a firm instruction more directly than a formal command — Te vas ahora mismo. None of these change the verb's form; only context tells you which job it's doing.

Cada forma, todos los nombres

Reference

1 · Presente simple (indicativo)

The default, unmarked present — one word, imperfective aspect, indicative mood.

Todos los nombres

Presente de indicativoRAE
PresenteAndrés Bello
Simple PresentPedagógica
Escribo un informe.
I write a report. / I am writing a report.

Uso: universal truths, habitual actions, present states, immediate scheduled future, and the historical narrative present.

Watch for overlap: habitual meaning here can look identical in translation to the perífrasis habitual (soler + infinitivo) below — the difference is that the simple present carries habitual meaning on its own, with no auxiliary verb.

2 · Presente perfecto (indicativo)

A compound form — haber conjugated in the present + participio — expressing perfective/resultative aspect.

Todos los nombres

Pretérito perfecto compuesto de indicativoRAE, oficial
Presente perfecto de indicativoPedagógica / gramática moderna
Antepresente de indicativoAndrés Bello
Pretérito perfectoUso tradicional europeo
Present PerfectPedagógica (inglés)
He terminado la reunión hoy.
I have finished the meeting today.

Uso (norma peninsular): actions completed within a psychological or temporal frame that includes the present (hoy, este año, nunca, ya), or completed past actions with current relevance.

This is the form where dialect matters most. The description above is the Peninsular norm. In most of Latin America — Mexico and the Río de la Plata especially — He terminado la reunión hoy sounds odd, and speakers say Terminé la reunión hoy instead. See the dialect note at the bottom of this tab before you assume either pattern is "the rule."

3 · Presente progresivo / continuo

Estar conjugated in the present + gerundio — durative aspect, action captured mid-unfolding.

Todos los nombres

Presente continuo / presente progresivoUso general
Perífrasis durativa de presente (estar + gerundio)Descriptiva
Aspecto cursivo / durativo en presenteTerminología aspectual
Present Progressive / ContinuousPedagógica (inglés)
Estoy calificando los exámenes.
I am grading the exams.

Uso: an action actively unfolding in real time at the precise moment of speech, or an ongoing, temporary present process.

Watch for overlap: both this form and the perífrasis acumulativa (llevar/venir + gerundio) use a gerundio — the difference is the auxiliary verb and the meaning it contributes: estar = happening right now; llevar/venir = accumulating duration up to now.

4 · Presente de subjuntivo

Same time-frame as the indicative present, but filtered through the subjunctive mood.

6 · Perífrasis aspectuales de presente

Verbal periphrases that use an inflected present-tense auxiliary to add a specific aspectual nuance the plain tenses above can't express on their own. Each one is built as auxiliar conjugado + (conector) + infinitivo / gerundio.

Aspecto prospectivo

Perífrasis prospectiva (ir a + infinitivo)RAE / NGLE
Perífrasis de futuro próximoPedagógica
Futuro perifrásticoUso tradicional
Voy a comenzar la clase.
I am going to begin class.

Uso: posteriority — an action located after the present moment, with intention or likelihood already in view now.

A correction worth knowing: many textbooks label ir a + infinitivo incoativa or ingresiva. That's inaccurate. Incoative periphrases mark the onset of an action already beginning — ponerse a, echarse a, romper a, empezar a (Se puso a llover = it started raining). Ir a + infinitivo marks posteriority: the action hasn't started at all. The NGLE keeps these separate, and so should you.

Aspecto terminativo / perfectivo reciente

Perífrasis de pasado inmediato / terminativa (acabar de + infinitivo)
Acabo de enviar el correo.
I just sent / have just sent the email.

Uso: an action completed immediately before the present instant.

Aspecto habitual / frecuentativo / iterativo

Perífrasis frecuentativa / de hábito (soler + infinitivo / acostumbrar a + infinitivo)
Suelo revisar mis notas.
I usually review my notes.

Uso: recurring, customary present routines.

Aspecto durativo acumulativo

Perífrasis acumulativa / continuativa (llevar + gerundio / venir + gerundio)
Llevo enseñando veinte años.
I have been teaching for twenty years.

Uso: an action originating in the past that persists uninterrupted and accumulates duration up to the exact present.

Frequency note: llevar + gerundio is far more common in speech than venir + gerundio. Venir + gerundio adds a sense of gradual development toward the present (Vengo notando un cambio — I've been gradually noticing a change) and leans formal.

Aspecto continuativo

Perífrasis continuativa (seguir + gerundio / continuar + gerundio)RAE / NGLE
Perífrasis durativa continuativaTerminología aspectual
Sigo trabajando en el proyecto.
I'm still working on the project.

Uso: an action that was already underway and has not stopped — the emphasis is on non-interruption, often against an expectation that it might have ended. Seguir is the everyday choice; continuar is the more formal register.

Very high frequency in speech. Seguir + gerundio is one of the most common periphrases in spoken Spanish, and it is often the natural equivalent of English "still" — which learners tend to render with the adverb todavía plus a plain present. Both work, but Sigo trabajando is more idiomatic than Todavía trabajo here.

Aspecto durativo coloquial / frecuentativo

Perífrasis durativa (andar + gerundio)RAE / NGLE
Perífrasis frecuentativa coloquialDescriptiva
Anda diciendo que se va a mudar.
He's going around saying he's going to move.

Uso: a durative action with a scattered, repeated, or unfocused quality — spread across time rather than happening at one instant. Frequently carries a mild connotation of disapproval or gossip.

Why this one is worth knowing: andar + gerundio is markedly frequent in Mexican and Central American speech and almost absent from textbooks. It is unmistakably colloquial — students will hear it constantly and rarely see it written. Contrast Está diciendo (saying it right now) with Anda diciendo (going around saying it repeatedly).
Variación dialectal

España y América: el perfecto compuesto

The single biggest regional split in the present-tense system is how the compound perfect (he hablado) divides labor with the simple preterite (hablé).

Peninsular Spanish (most of Spain): the compound perfect covers hodiernal past — anything within today, or within a time frame the speaker still counts as current. Hoy he comido tarde. Este año he viajado mucho. ¿Ya has terminado? Using the preterite there would sound like the event has been pushed away into a closed past.

Mexican, Central American, Caribbean, and Rioplatense Spanish: the simple preterite takes over nearly all of that territory. Hoy comí tarde. Este año viajé mucho. ¿Ya terminaste? The compound perfect survives mainly for continuative or iterative readings — actions that began in the past and are still going, or that have happened repeatedly: He vivido aquí diez años. He ido a Oaxaca varias veces.

Andean Spanish (Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, NW Argentina): patterns differently again, with the compound perfect extended into some evidential and narrative uses not found in either of the above.

What this means for this class: none of these is more correct than the others. If your Spanish comes from a mission, a family, or a country, your intuition here is data, not error — and it may legitimately conflict with what a grammar book or a Spain-published textbook says. What matters for an intermediate conversation course is that you can recognize both systems when you hear them, and that you're internally consistent within your own variety.

Variación dialectal

Otras diferencias que afectan al presente

Vosotros vs. ustedes: Spain distinguishes informal plural vosotros habláis from formal ustedes hablan. All of Latin America uses ustedes for both, so the vosotros forms simply don't appear in the paradigm — one fewer form to conjugate, in every tense on this page.

Voseo: in Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, and much of Central America, vos replaces tú with its own present-tense endings: vos hablás, vos comés, vos vivís (stress on the final syllable, no stem change — vos podés, not vos puedes). The present subjunctive varies regionally: Rioplatense typically uses the tú form (que vos hables), while Central American voseo often keeps a distinct que vos hablés.

Progressive frequency: the estar + gerundio progressive is used more freely in Latin American Spanish, and its use has expanded further in U.S. Spanish under contact with English. Peninsular Spanish more often prefers the plain simple present where an English speaker would reach for a progressive.

Periphrasis preferences: andar + gerundio is strongly Mexican/Central American. Acostumbrar a + infinitivo is more common in Latin America than in Spain, where soler dominates. And in casual Latin American speech, ir a + infinitivo has largely displaced the synthetic future (voy a ir over iré).

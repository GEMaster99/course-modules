El imperfecto: verbos regulares

Spanish has two ways to talk about the past. The preterit narrates completed actions — what happened. The imperfect describes: repeated or habitual actions in the past, actions that were ongoing, and what things used to be like.

Look for time-cue words that signal the imperfect: siempre, todos los días, todos los años, con frecuencia, usualmente, normalmente, a veces.

Pronombre-AR: chatear-ER/-IR: leer
yochateabaleía
túchateabasleías
él/ella/ustedchateabaleía
nosotroschateábamosleíamos
vosotroschateabaisleíais
ellos/ustedeschateabanleían

-AR verbs carry only one accent mark, on the nosotros form. -ER/-IR verbs carry an accented í in every single form.

Examples: Cuando era niña, no había móviles. (When I was a little girl, there were no cellphones.) Yo chateaba por internet mientras mi hermano montaba en bicicleta. (I chatted online while my brother rode a bike.)

Good news for irregular verbs: the imperfect has no stem changes at all. Poder (puedo in the present) becomes a perfectly regular podía, podías, podía… and tener (tengo) becomes tenía, tenías, tenía… The imperfect flattens out present-tense irregularities like these.
Watch out for haber: hay (present), hubo (preterit), and había (imperfect) are each used only in the singular form, no matter what follows — había móviles, not habían móviles.

Ser, ir y ver en el imperfecto

Only three verbs are irregular in the imperfect: ser, ir, and ver. Every other verb in Spanish — regular or irregular in other tenses — follows the regular pattern you just saw.

PronombreSerIrVer
yoeraibaveía
túerasibasveías
él/ella/ustederaibaveía
nosotroséramosíbamosveíamos
vosotroseraisibaisveíais
ellos/ustedeseranibanveían

Notice that ver keeps the e from its stem (ve-) and just adds the regular -ER/-IR imperfect endings — the only irregularity is that it doesn't drop down to a plain "ía", it stays "veía". Ser and ir are the only truly irregular stems.

Memory tip: Only three verbs, and all three describe things that "just were" — who you were (ser), where you went habitually (ir), and what you used to watch (ver) — fittingly, for a module about television.

Palabras indefinidas y negativas

Indefinite words (something, someone, always) each pair with a negative counterpart (nothing, no one, never). If a question uses the indefinite word, a negative answer uses its negative partner.

Palabras indefinidasPalabras negativas
alguien (someone/anyone)nadie (no one)
algo (something)nada (nothing)
siempre (always)nunca / jamás (never)
también (also)tampoco (neither/either)
algún (alguno), alguna, algunos, algunas (some)ningún (ninguno), ninguna, ningunos, ningunas (none/any)
o…o (either…or)ni…ni (neither…nor)

Example: ¿Alguien tiene un MP3? (Does anyone have an MP3 player?) → Nadie tiene un MP3. (No one has an MP3 player.)

Unlike English, Spanish allows — and often requires — double negatives. If a negative word comes after the verb, you still need no (or another negative word) in front of the verb: No juego videojuegos tampoco. (I don't play videogames either.) But nadie, nunca, and tampoco can also go directly before the verb, acting as the subject, with no no needed: Nadie compra cosas en una tienda hoy en día. (No one buys things in a store these days.) Nada es fácil. (Nothing is easy.)

Algún/alguno and ningún/ninguno must agree in number and gender with the noun they describe, and they shorten (apocopate) to algún / ningún right before a singular masculine noun: ningún correo, not ninguno correo. When you drop the noun entirely, use ninguno or ninguna on their own — and note that the negative is almost always kept singular, even when talking about a plural idea: No tengo ningún libro en mi Kindle. / No tengo ninguno.

Try it: Tengo algunos libros en mi Kindle. ¿Y tú? — No, no tengo ningún libro en mi Kindle.

01 · Wonder · 8 minutes

From opinion to inquiry

What do you genuinely want to understand about AI and college learning?

“One thing I don’t understand about AI and college learning is…”
  1. Think and write · 2 min: Finish the sentence with a real uncertainty. Write in your Google Doc.
  2. Partner talk · 4 min: Explain your question. What makes it interesting? What does your partner wonder?
  3. Share · 2 min: Offer one question for our class list.

Possible territory: writing feedback, language learning, trust, course rules, access, confidence, or dependence.

Instructor notes

Collect 4–6 questions. Ask “What would we need to find out?” Keep questions open rather than answering them now.

02 · Project · 10 minutes

Join a conversation. Contribute evidence.

Build on rhetorical analysis

You examined how a text persuades an audience.

Now investigate a question and build your own evidence-based argument for an audience.

The destination

  • Approximately 1,900 words.
  • 6+ sources, including primary survey data.
  • Final paper: December 1.
  • A five-minute research presentation follows.

The route

Question → Sources + survey → Findings → Argument → Talk

You may return to your question as you read. Changing your mind can be evidence that your research is working.

Discuss: What makes this different from an opinion essay?

Reveal a useful distinction

An opinion gives a position. A researched argument explains how evidence supports a position, addresses complications, and shows why its conclusion matters to an audience.

Instructor notes

Introduce the destination without assigning every component today. The full draft is planned for November 20. Today’s product is a question and project seed.

03 · Two paths · 12 minutes

What does your starting point let you see?

Researcher A

“Professors should ban AI.”

Begins by seeking evidence to support this position.

Researcher B

“How do students decide which kinds of AI assistance are acceptable?”

Begins by investigating a question.

  1. Pairs · 4 min: What would each researcher search for or ask?
  2. Pairs · 3 min: What might each overlook?
  3. Class · 5 min: What finding could make each reconsider?
Reveal the comparison

A may seek examples of cheating and overlook useful applications or differences among tasks.

B can investigate conflicting definitions, policy confusion, and differences among uses. B still needs neutral questions and varied evidence.

A tentative position can guide research. The problem is refusing to consider evidence that challenges it.

Instructor notes

Ask students to invent one finding that would complicate each researcher’s thinking. Connect to Chapter 15: inquiry leaves room for research to change understanding.

04 · Narrow · 10 minutes

Make the question answerable.

Broad territory: AI and education

↓

Specific practice: AI feedback on student writing

↓

Focused inquiry: Under what conditions do multilingual students at WSU find AI writing feedback useful or unhelpful?

Published sources can explain…

Feedback, revision, language learning, and strengths and limitations of AI feedback.

Our survey can describe…

Respondents’ experiences, decisions, and perceived usefulness.

Research question or survey question?

Research question: The larger question your project investigates.

Survey question: One question asked of participants to gather evidence. Example: “Describe a time you accepted or rejected AI feedback. What influenced your decision?”

Reported usefulness does not, by itself, prove improved learning.

Instructor notes

Model the same process with a question from the board. Ask: Who? Which practice? Where? What uncertainty? This is a model, not a required topic.

05 · Develop · 15 minutes

Build three possible questions.

  1. Explore · 4 min: Make a topic map connecting AI and academic life to experiences, tensions, and uncertainties.
  2. Draft · 6 min: Write three genuinely different research questions in your Google Doc.
  3. Choose and narrow · 5 min: Select one. Identify the people, practice, setting, and uncertainty.
Try a question shape:

How do [people] decide [specific choice]?

Under what conditions do [people] find [practice] helpful or problematic?

What explains the gap between [expectation] and [reported experience]?

Stuck? Explore a tension.

Help versus dependence · Clear rules versus ambiguous rules · Correct grammar versus personal voice · Access versus unequal access · Confidence versus accuracy. Turn a tension into an uncertainty you can investigate.

Instructor notes

Confer with students whose questions are too broad, assume a conclusion, or require inaccessible evidence. Ask what evidence could challenge their assumptions.

06 · Clinic · 13 minutes

The question clinic

Four minutes per researcher. One final minute to revise.

Each four-minute round

  1. 1 min: Researcher explains the question and uncertainty.
  2. 2 min: Partners test scope and evidence.
  3. 1 min: Researcher rewrites the question.

Rotate until everyone has been the researcher.

Five pressure tests

  • What do you still need to discover?
  • Are the people and setting clear?
  • What published research would help?
  • What could a small survey contribute?
  • Can you investigate this before the draft?
“Your question seems to assume…”
“You could narrow it by…”
“Evidence that might complicate your thinking is…”
Test: “Why should all professors ban AI?”

It assumes both the action and its scope. Investigate which uses, tasks, and conditions create concerns before deciding what professors should do.

Instructor notes

Call rotations at four and eight minutes; signal a final revision at twelve. Listen for partners improving the question rather than simply answering it.

07 · Project seed · 7 minutes

Leave with a project seed.

Write a short project seed in your Google Doc. This begins your research memo.

  1. Question: My research question is…
  2. Scope: The people, practice, and setting I am examining are…
  3. Audience: This matters to [audience] because…
  4. Assumptions: My current thinking is… Evidence that could challenge it is…
  5. Evidence: Published sources could help me understand… A small survey could help me understand…
  6. Uncertainty: One thing I still need to figure out is…
Language move: embedded questions

Direct: “Why do students use AI?”

Embedded: “This project investigates why students use AI.”

ENGL 1010 · AI AND COLLEGE LEARNING

From questions to a researched argument

Investigate an issue. Weigh the evidence. Develop your own defensible answer. Our class shares a survey; each writer builds an individual argument.

Use the buttons or the left/right arrow keys. Steps remain visible as you advance.

1 · Individual exploration

Explore the issue and ask a focused question

Use our guided activities to explore AI in college learning, then draft your own research question. Start with something you can investigate, rather than a position you must prove.

Example: Under what conditions should writing courses permit AI feedback on student-written drafts?
2 · Published evidence

Find and understand credible sources

Find research and reliable reporting that address your question. Identify each source’s main claim, evidence, and limitations. Look for more than one perspective.

Today: understand at least one source well. Build toward at least six sources, including three scholarly sources.
3 · Source practice

Summarize, paraphrase, and cite

Explain source ideas accurately in your own words, add in-text citations, and record full reference details. Discuss what each source helps you understand.

Ask: What does this evidence establish? What does it leave unanswered?
4 · Shared class inquiry

Choose our shared survey focus

Bring individual questions and source insights into class discussion. Your instructor will establish one shared question about students’ AI experiences and opinions that connects with our individual investigations.

One shared survey question does not mean one required thesis.
5 · Shared survey

Design neutral survey questions

Develop clear questions about reported practices, experiences, and opinions. Avoid leading wording and include reasonable response options.

Ask about a specific use, such as AI feedback, rather than treating every AI activity as the same.
6 · Local evidence

Collect and interpret pooled responses

Collect responses, combine the class data, and examine patterns and disagreements. Record who responded, how participants were recruited, and the sample size.

Write “Among the students we surveyed…” A convenience sample does not represent all Weber State students or establish learning effects.

7 · Individual synthesis

Weigh the evidence and develop a working thesis

Use published research as your main evidence. Select relevant survey findings to add local perspectives. They may support, complicate, or challenge your position.

Develop a qualified answer to your individual question. If evidence is missing or contradictory, return to research and reconsider your answer.
8 · Individual argument

Organize by reasons and address objections

Introduce the issue and thesis. Develop reasons with evidence and your explanation. Address a credible counterargument and explain why your position remains reasonable.

Briefly explain your survey methods and limitations where useful. Organize the essay around the argument rather than a sequence of source summaries or survey results.
9 · Review and revision

Test your argument with readers

Use peer review and instructor feedback to strengthen reasoning, source integration, organization, and qualifications. Check citations and revise the thesis when the evidence calls for it.

Ask: Does each paragraph explain how the evidence supports a reason? Have I treated competing views fairly?
10 · Final submission

Submit the argument and reflect on the process

Submit your revised researched argument, approximately 1,900 words, with complete source documentation and a brief process reflection, following the assignment directions.

Explain how your research shaped or changed your position.
The organizing center is your argument. Published research provides the main evidence; the informal campus survey contributes local experiences and opinions. Research is recursive: return to earlier steps whenever new evidence changes your thinking.
